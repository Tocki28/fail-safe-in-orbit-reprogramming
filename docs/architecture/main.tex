\section{Purpose and Scope}
This document records the software architecture of the System: its
components, what each does, how they connect, and the design decisions behind
them [1,~Sec.~4.2.3, p.~33]. It covers the three software items of the
requirements [6,~Sec.~3.1].

The architecture concentrates on ``the most pressing risks'' [3,~Sec.~4]: the
eight catastrophic and critical hazards that do not yet meet the fault
tolerance rule [5,~Sec.~5.0].

It names no hardware. It says what each component does, not which device does
it, and ends with what the hardware must provide. The detailed design of each
component, with its algorithms and data, is recorded before that component is
coded [1,~Sec.~4.3.2, p.~34].

\noindent\textbf{How to read it.} Section~3.0 shows every component on one
figure. Section~6.0 holds the design decisions, each with the options weighed
and the hazards it targets. Section~7.0 describes the components, and
Appendix~A shows which component meets each requirement. Section~9.0 lists
what the hardware must provide.

\section{System Overview}
The System has three software items [6,~Sec.~3.1], each made of components
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). The ground software prepares and signs what the
operator decides, and keeps what comes back. The onboard computer software
checks everything that arrives, holds it, passes it on, and watches and
restores the controller. The controller firmware runs the controller: a
start-up selector chooses which firmware copy runs, the main firmware does the
work, and the last-resort firmware takes over when the main firmware cannot.

\section{Constraints}
The architecture works within the constraints and assumptions of the ConOps
[4,~Sec.~1.1.2] and its accepted risks [4,~Sec.~8.0]. Those below shape it.
\begin{description}
\item[C-1] Every command, report and firmware image passes through the onboard
  computer, so the onboard computer is on every path between the ground and the
  controller.
\item[C-3] One engineer builds the System. Independence between components
  comes from separate code, not from separate teams.
\item[A-2] Power can be lost at any moment, including while memory is being
  written.
\item[A-5] Bit errors can change stored data and held commands.
\item[A-6] The onboard computer's own software, including its operating system,
  is corrected by a system outside this project.
\item[R-4] There is one onboard computer. Its permanent loss stops commands,
  reports and restores. The risk is accepted, so H-4 cannot meet the fault
  tolerance rule [5,~Sec.~5.2].
\item[C-4] The System runs on two given development boards. The design does not
  depend on them; they are checked against it at the end of this document.
\end{description}

\section{Software Qualities}
The software qualities [1,~Sec.~4.2.3, p.~33] that drive this design are the
two a high-risk project emphasises, fault tolerance and security
[3,~Sec.~4], and the testability the requirements demand.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.4cm]
\item[Fault tolerance] Every catastrophic hazard needs at least three failures
  together to occur, and every critical hazard two [5,~Sec.~3.3].
\item[Security] Only the operator's commands and firmware are acted on (O-2).
\item[Testability] Each safety-critical component can be tested on its own on
  the bench, including under injected faults, to full modified
  condition/decision coverage [6,~REQ-108].
\end{description}

\section{Design Decisions}
Each decision targets hazards that do not yet meet the fault tolerance rule
[5,~Sec.~5.2]. It states the problem, weighs the options, and lists the
requirements the choice adds; these enter the requirements at their next
revision.

Options are compared by the smallest number of failures that, together, cause
the hazard with that option in place. The counts leave out the permanent loss
of the onboard computer, an accepted risk (R-4). They are design targets, which
the hazard analysis confirms in its design phase [5,~Sec.~1.0]. Chosen: YES or
NO.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6.1
\subsection{Start-Up and Fall-Back}
\field{Problem} Every firmware copy, including the one the restore puts back,
shares the start-up code and the data kept over restarts. One defect there
stops them all, and the restore brings it back [5,~Sec.~5.2]. Targets H-1 and
H-8.

\smallskip
{\small\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xccc@{}}
\toprule
 & Option & H-1 & H-8 & Chosen \\
\midrule
a & The main firmware runs first and chooses its copy; the restore puts back a stored copy of it & 2 & 1 & NO \\
b & The last-resort firmware runs first and chooses; the restore puts it back & 2 & 1 & NO \\
c & A start-up selector, sharing no code with either firmware, runs first and chooses; the restore puts the last-resort firmware in its place & 3 & 2 & YES \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\field{Choice} Option c \tbr{startup}. A defect in the start-up selector, or
one in both main firmware copies, no longer stops the controller: the
last-resort firmware shares no code with them [6,~REQ-009] and runs in their
place.

\field{Adds}
\begin{itemize}
\item The start-up selector shall share no code with the main firmware or the
  last-resort firmware.
\item The restore shall put the last-resort firmware in the start-up
  selector's place, so that it runs first.
\item The onboard computer software shall put back the start-up selector and
  the main firmware through the reprogramming link when the operator orders it.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6.2
\subsection{The Way Back In}
\field{Problem} A restore that goes through any controller software fails
whenever that software fails, which the requirements forbid [6,~REQ-056].
Targets H-1, H-3 and H-4.

\smallskip
{\small\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xcc@{}}
\toprule
 & Option & H-1 & Chosen \\
\midrule
a & The restore is a command that the controller's firmware carries out & 2 & NO \\
b & The reprogramming link loads the controller's memory whatever it holds; the onboard computer drives it and can restart the controller & 3 & YES \\
c & Two such links, independent of each other & 4 & NO \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\field{Choice} Option b \tbr{wayback}. One link meets the rule; a second adds
a connection to build and to check between uses [6,~REQ-059] for a margin the
rule does not ask for.

\field{Adds}
\begin{itemize}
\item The reprogramming link shall load the controller's memory whatever that
  memory holds.
\item The onboard computer software shall be able to restart the controller.
\item No controller firmware shall be able to disable the reprogramming link.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6.3
\subsection{Restore Apart From Update}
\field{Problem} The update path and the restore share onboard computer
software and stored firmware. One fault there stops both until the outside
system corrects it (A-6) [5,~Sec.~5.2]. Targets H-3.

\smallskip
{\small\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xcc@{}}
\toprule
 & Option & H-3 & Chosen \\
\midrule
a & One program updates and restores, from one firmware store & 2 & NO \\
b & A restore program, separate from the update manager, with its own firmware store & 3 & YES \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\field{Choice} Option b \tbr{apart}. New firmware can still reach the
controller through the restore program, which loads firmware from the ground
[6,~REQ-058], when the update path fails.

\field{Adds}
\begin{itemize}
\item The restore program shall share no code or stored data with the update
  manager.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6.4
\subsection{Proof of Origin}
\field{Problem} One means proves that both commands and firmware come from the
operator. If it is disclosed, another party can command the controller and
load firmware [5,~Sec.~5.2]. Targets H-2 and H-5.

\smallskip
{\small\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xccc@{}}
\toprule
 & Option & H-2 & H-5 & Chosen \\
\midrule
a & One secret, held on the ground and on board, checks commands and firmware & 1 & 1 & NO \\
b & Digital signatures made with one key: on board is only the means to check them, so the key can be disclosed only on the ground & 1 & 1 & NO \\
c & Signatures made with two keys kept in two separate places; the radio link's own protection, outside this project, as a third barrier & 2 & 2 & NO \\
d & Signatures made with three keys kept in three separate places; every command and firmware image carries all three & 3 & 3 & YES \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\field{Choice} Option d \tbr{origin}. Each disclosure exposes one key, so
another party needs three. The controller checks firmware signatures again
before running it [6,~REQ-006], with code different from the onboard
computer's, so that one defect in that code cannot pass forged firmware
through both checks [8,~Sec.~7.4.2, p.~141].

\field{Adds}
\begin{itemize}
\item Every command and firmware image shall carry signatures made with three
  keys kept in three separate places on the ground.
\item The onboard computer software shall act on a command or firmware image
  only when all three signatures are valid.
\item The controller firmware shall check firmware signatures with code
  different from the onboard computer software's.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6.5
\subsection{Two Clocks}
\field{Problem} Expiry, arm time-outs, scheduled commands, time-tags and the
trial time limit all depend on the onboard clock. A wrong clock alone runs a
scheduled command at the wrong time and gives reports wrong times
[5,~Sec.~5.2]. Targets H-6 and H-9.

\smallskip
{\small\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xccc@{}}
\toprule
 & Option & H-6 & H-9 & Chosen \\
\midrule
a & The onboard clock alone, compared with ground time at each contact [6,~REQ-072] & 1 & 1 & NO \\
b & Counts instead of times wherever possible, such as report and command numbers & 1 & 1 & NO \\
c & A second clock in the controller, with its own time source, set only from ground time; each processor makes every time check with its own clock, and every report carries both times & 2 & 2 & YES \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\field{Choice} Option c \tbr{clocks}. A wrong clock on one processor is caught
by the other. The controller's clock is never set from the onboard clock, so
one wrong value cannot reach both.

\field{Adds}
\begin{itemize}
\item The controller firmware shall keep its own clock, from a time source
  separate from the onboard computer's.
\item The controller's clock shall be set only from ground time.
\item The controller firmware shall refuse a command past its expiry, by its
  own clock.
\item The controller firmware shall run a scheduled command only when its own
  clock agrees with the scheduled time within \tbd{agree}.
\item Every report shall carry the time of both clocks.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6.6
\subsection{Held Data}
\field{Problem} A bit error can change a held command, the arm state or the
kept data after its check and before its use (A-5) [5,~Sec.~5.2]. Targets H-6.

\smallskip
{\small\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xcc@{}}
\toprule
 & Option & H-6 & Chosen \\
\midrule
a & Each item is checked once, when it arrives & 1 & NO \\
b & Each held item is stored with a check value and checked again just before use; the arm state is stored as a value that no single bit error turns into armed & 2 & YES \\
c & Memory that corrects bit errors on its own & 2 & NO \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\field{Choice} Option b \tbr{held}. A bit error must also produce a matching
check value, a second failure. Unlike option c, it needs no hardware feature;
option c can be added where the hardware has it.

\field{Adds}
\begin{itemize}
\item Every held command and the kept data shall be stored with a check value
  and checked just before use.
\item The arm state shall be stored so that no single bit error makes it armed.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6.7
\subsection{Two Kinds of Connection}
\field{Problem} The two data-link connections share software at both ends, so
one defect cuts both (R-1) [5,~Sec.~5.2]. Targets H-4.

\smallskip
{\small\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xcc@{}}
\toprule
 & Option & Failures to cut both & Chosen \\
\midrule
a & Two connections of one kind, one driver at each end & 1 & NO \\
b & Two connections of one kind, a separate driver for each & 1 & NO \\
c & Two different kinds of connection, a separate driver for each & 2 & YES \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\field{Choice} Option c \tbr{links}. One defect, or one fault common to a kind
of connection, now cuts one connection; the other carries on (O-3). H-4 still
falls to the loss of the onboard computer (R-4).

\field{Adds}
\begin{itemize}
\item The two data-link connections shall be of different kinds.
\end{itemize}
