\section{Purpose and Scope}
This document is the preliminary hazard analysis of the System: its hazards,
their causes and severity, and the controls proposed against them.

The hazard analysis is delivered once per safety phase, each closed by a
safety review [2,~Secs.~3.1 and 4]:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.6cm]
\item[Phase 0] Concept complete: the top hazards and their causes.
\item[Phase 1] Requirements nearly complete: all known hazards, with their
  causes, controls and risk.
\item[Phase 2] Design mostly complete: the approved controls, and how each is
  verified.
\item[Phase 3] Testing complete: proof that the verifications passed.
\end{description}
This document is the first delivery: phases 0 and 1, which small projects
combine [2,~Sec.~4]. The controls are proposals that the requirements turn
into rules; each already names its verification method
[7,~App.~F.3, p.~348].

It covers the software items of the Software Classification [6,~Sec.~3.0] in
operation: all controller modes, during and between contacts. Bench and build
tools are covered where they can reach operation. Its hazards decide which
software is safety-critical; the Software Classification records that
designation [6,~Sec.~4.0].

\noindent\textbf{How to read it.} Section~4.0 lists every hazard, how severe
it is, and whether it is tolerant enough. Section~5.0 explains in plain words
why some are not, and what has to change. Section~6.0 gives one report per
catastrophic or critical hazard. The appendices hold the analyses behind them:
the fault tree, the failure tables, the glossary and the open items.

\section{Ground Rules}
These rules set the boundary, the failures included, the fault tolerance
required and how failures are modelled and named, before the analysis starts
[3,~Sec.~3.1; 4,~pp.~23--24].

\subsection{Boundary}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Harm:} the loss of the controller's operability, observability
  or correctability.
\item \textbf{Boundary:} the radio equipment and the spacecraft power supply lie
  outside the System [5,~Sec.~1.2.2]. Their failures enter as causes and are
  not analysed further.
\item \textbf{Physical tampering:} excluded; nobody can reach the spacecraft
  (Assumption A-1).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis Rules}
This section defines the evaluation rules, fault tolerance criteria, and
baseline assumptions governing the formal hazard and fault tree analyses
[3,~Sec.~3.1; 7,~Sec.~3.2.1].

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Counting failures:} to check a hazard, we count the failures
  that would have to happen for it to occur. If one cause disables several
  controls at once, it counts as one failure, not one per control. Counting
  each control separately would make the System look more tolerant than it is.
\item \textbf{Severity:} each hazard is rated by how bad its outcome is if it
  happens, using the four categories in the legend below. The rating ignores
  the controls: they make a hazard less likely, but its outcome is just as bad
  when it does happen. A hazard therefore keeps its severity however many
  controls it has.
\item \textbf{Fault tolerance:} how many failures must happen together before a
  hazard can occur. A catastrophic hazard must need at least three of the
  failures in Section~3.2 together, a critical hazard at least two
  [3,~Sec.~3.1]. For each hazard, we find the smallest set of
  failures that causes it and compare it with this number. A hazard that needs
  fewer breaks the rule, and its controls must be strengthened.
\item \textbf{Safety effort:} full [7,~Sec.~3.2.1, p.~44], because between
  contacts no operator can respond to a hazard (A-3). It decides the analyses
  here: a fault tree is mandatory and a failure modes analysis highly
  recommended [7,~Table~6-3, p.~107].
\item \textbf{Assumptions:} the analysis relies on the ConOps assumptions A-1 to
  A-5, and adds two of its own, each taken as the worst case:
  \begin{enumerate}[nosep, leftmargin=1.8em]
  \item the last-resort firmware the restore puts back has the same start-up
    code, defects and kept data as the other images, so a cause that stops
    every image also stops the restored one;
  \item one means proves that both commands and firmware come from the
    operator, and both the onboard computer and the controller use it, so
    disclosing it opens both.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Legend: severity} (categories from [7,~Table~2-2, p.~27])
\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.9cm]
\item[CATASTROPHIC] The controller is lost for the rest of the mission: it
  cannot run, or can no longer be commanded, observed or corrected.
\item[CRITICAL] The controller acts against the operator's intent, or cannot be
  commanded, observed or corrected until the operator acts at a later contact.
\item[MODERATE] Part of a function is lost, such as some health reports or one
  update attempt; the controller still runs and obeys the operator.
\item[NEGLIGIBLE] Nothing is lost; the operator has extra work or gets data a
  contact later.
\end{description}

\subsection{Naming}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Hazards:} H-1 to H-17, in order of severity.
\item \textbf{Fault tree:} gates G01 to G06; primary events B01 to B10. No
  identifier is ever reused.
\item \textbf{ConOps numbers:} objectives (O), assumptions (A), constraints
  (C), risks (R), capabilities and scenarios refer to the ConOps
  [5,~Secs.~1.1.2, 3.1, 3.5, 6.0 and 8.0].
\item \textbf{Software role} [2,~Sec.~3.2.2]: CAUSE, a software fault can lead
  to the hazard; CONTROL, software prevents it; MITIGATION, software limits its
  effect; MONITOR, software detects and reports it.
\item \textbf{Verification method} [8,~Sec.~5.3.1.2.1, p.~92]:
  TEST, using the product to obtain detailed data;
  DEMONSTRATION, using it to show the control works, without
  detailed data; INSPECTION, examining the product or its
  records; ANALYSIS, modelling or calculation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Findings}
Eight of the twelve catastrophic and critical hazards do not meet the fault
tolerance rule yet: seven can happen from a single failure, and H-3 from two.

\subsection{What Causes Each Hazard}
The smallest set of failures that, happening together, causes each
catastrophic and critical hazard, with the proposed controls in place:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4cm]
\item[H-1] One failure: a fault that every firmware copy shares, including the
  one the restore puts back: the start-up code, the data kept over restarts, or
  a common defect (B08 to B10).
\item[H-2] One failure: the means of proving origin is disclosed; both checks
  rely on it.
\item[H-3] Two failures: a fault in the onboard computer's update path or its
  stored data, and its correction by the system outside this project failing
  (A-6).
\item[H-4] One failure: a software defect that cuts both data-link
  connections, or the onboard computer failing for good.
\item[H-5] One failure: the means of proving origin is disclosed.
\item[H-6] One failure: a wrong onboard clock runs a scheduled command at the
  wrong time, or a bit error changes a held command after its check.
\item[H-7] Two failures: a hidden defect in new firmware, and a trial that does
  not exercise it.
\item[H-8] One failure: the same defect in every firmware copy; every restart,
  and the restore, brings it back.
\item[H-9] One failure: a wrong onboard clock gives every report a wrong time.
\item[H-10] Two failures: a fault, and fault detection that misses it.
\item[H-11] Two failures: a command that is lost, and a ground that does not
  notice its answer never came.
\item[H-12] Two failures: fault-injection functions left in the operating
  software, and a wrong command that starts them.
\end{description}

\subsection{Shared Causes That Break Fault Tolerance}
Each hazard above that breaks the rule does so through a cause that several
controls share: one event defeats them all at once. The analysis looked for such shared causes,
including the same defect in software that runs in more than one place
[1,~App.~A.1.1, p.~62]. Each is listed with the hazards it reaches and what the
requirements must change.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Shared image code and data.} Every firmware copy, including the
  last-resort firmware the restore puts back, shares the start-up code, the
  data kept over restarts and any defect they have in common (assumption~1).
  One fault there stops them all. Breaks H-1 and H-8; also reaches H-6 and
  H-13. \textit{To change:} the last-resort firmware and the restore must not
  depend on what the other copies share.
\item \textbf{One proof of origin.} One means proves that both commands and
  firmware come from the operator (assumption~2). If it is disclosed, another
  party can command the controller and load firmware. Breaks H-2 and H-5.
  \textit{To change:} a disclosure must be noticed, so the means can be
  replaced in flight (Scenario-370).
\item \textbf{Onboard computer software.} It is corrected by a system outside
  this project (A-6), so a defect in its update path stays until that system
  corrects it. Breaks H-3, which then needs two failures; also reaches H-1 and
  H-4. \textit{Outside this project:} the outside system must be able to
  correct the onboard computer's update path and stored data.
\item \textbf{One onboard computer.} It is the only path for commands, reports
  and restores; if it fails for good, all three stop (R-4). Breaks H-4; also
  reaches H-1, H-8 and H-10. \textit{Outside this project:} how many onboard
  computers the spacecraft carries is a spacecraft decision; the risk is
  accepted (R-4).
\item \textbf{Same data-link software.} The two data-link connections share
  software at both ends, so one defect cuts both (R-1) [1,~p.~66]. Breaks H-4.
  \textit{To change:} one defect must not be able to cut both connections.
\item \textbf{Wrong onboard clock.} Expiry, arm time-outs, scheduled commands,
  time-tags and the trial time limit all depend on it [1,~p.~65]. A wrong
  clock alone runs a scheduled command at the wrong time and gives reports
  wrong times. Breaks H-6 and H-9; also reaches H-4, H-11 and H-13.
  \textit{To change:} no check may depend on the onboard clock alone.
\item \textbf{Radiation.} A bit error can change a held command after it was
  checked and before it runs (A-5) [1,~p.~63]. Breaks H-6; bit errors also
  reach H-1, H-7, H-8, H-9 and H-10. \textit{To change:} a held command is
  checked again just before it runs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Other Shared Causes}
These defeat several controls at once, but no hazard falls to them alone.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One power supply.} It feeds both the onboard computer and the
  controller [5,~Sec.~3.3]; one loss stops the controller and the computer that
  would restore it. Reaches H-1, H-8, H-10, H-11 and H-15.
\item \textbf{One set of build tools.} The same tools build every firmware
  copy; a tool defect enters all of them. Reaches H-1, H-7 and H-12.
\end{itemize}

\section{Glossary}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.4cm]
\item[Common cause] A cause that defeats several controls at once; it can
  negate a redundancy [4,~Sec.~1.3, p.~3].
\item[Control] Hazard control: ``means of reducing the risk of exposure to a
  hazard'' [1,~p.~13].
\item[Fault tolerance] ``The ability to perform a function in the presence of
  any of a specified number of coincident, independent failure causes of
  specified types'' \mbox{[9,~pp.~18--19]}.
\item[Hazard] ``A state or a set of conditions, internal or external to a
  system that has the potential to cause harm'' [1,~p.~13].
\item[Minimal cut set] ``The smallest combination of basic events that result
  in the top event'' [4,~Sec.~1.3, p.~3].
\end{description}
